# Chapter Five — No Personnel File

The thing about a voice in your ear was that you never thought to ask where the mouth was.

Kane had been turning that over for two days, in the dead hours between taskings, in the gray apartment Meridian kept for him in a building where nobody met anyone's eyes in the elevator. It was the kind of thought that didn't announce itself. It crept up the way the cold had come through his boots in the Vault — from underneath, before anything else did. One morning he was making coffee he wouldn't drink and he caught himself wondering, idly, the way you wonder about an old scar, what Grace looked like.

And he realized he had no idea. Eight months. Eight months of her voice threaded through the worst nights of his recent life, calling patrols two minutes out, reading his heart rate back to him like a fortune teller reading a palm, and he could not have picked her out of a room of three women. He didn't know her last name. He didn't know if Grace was even a first name or just a word someone had handed him the way they handed him a bridge and a route and a building to climb.

He set the coffee down and went looking.

He did it the careful way, because careful was the only way he knew how to do anything anymore. He didn't ask. Asking left a shape in the record where a question had been, and questions got read by the wrong people. Instead he used the access he already had — the analyst credentials Voss had cut for him for the Astreon workup, the ones nobody had thought to claw back. He told himself it was housekeeping. He told himself a lot of things in the gray apartment.

Meridian's personnel directory was a quiet, ugly piece of software, federal-issue, the color of an old filing cabinet. He typed *Grace* into it and got eleven results. A Grace in logistics, sixty-one, retiring in spring. A Grace in legal counsel. A Grace who had died in 2027 and never been purged from the rolls. None of them had ever been near an operations channel. None of them had a voice he knew.

He tried it by his own file instead. Pulled his own jacket — KANE, SETH M., the photo three years stale, the Tallinn footnote scrubbed down to a single sealed line — and walked the assignment history down to the field-handler designation for the current operation. The field said: *ASSIGNED HANDLER — G.* 

Just the letter. A field that, on every other operative's jacket he'd ever glimpsed, held a name and a badge string and a desk extension you could call when the comms went sideways. On his, a single capital G, the way you'd label a folder you meant to fill in later and never did.

He sat with that for a while. Then he went to find the desk extension.

---

There wasn't one. There was a routing handle, an alphanumeric that pushed his query into a relay and the relay returned a green checkmark and nothing else. No org chart node behind it. No reporting line above it. He traced it up two levels and the third level was a permissions wall the color of every other permissions wall in the building, except this one didn't even pretend to have a door. It just said *INSUFFICIENT* and logged the attempt. He stopped before it logged a second one.

A handler with no jacket. No photo. No service history, no clearance dossier, no in-processing date, no emergency contact, no signature on the operation's authorizing memo where every other name was inked three times over. He went back and checked the memo twice. The handler line read *G.* in the same place his read *KANE, S.* He had a whole life pinned to a federal database, every wound and every black mark and the exact weight he'd been at his last physical. She had a letter.

It could mean she was deep cover. It could mean she was so far inside a compartment that even her compartment had a compartment. Meridian was the kind of place where that was a real answer and not a paranoid one. He'd worked with ghosts before; ghosts had paper somewhere, even if the paper lived in a safe in Hale's office with a number instead of a name. This was different. This wasn't a ghost. A ghost was a person you weren't allowed to see. This was a shape with no person in it at all.

He took the stairs down to tech-ops.

---

Voss had the corner of the floor with the good chair and the wall of dead monitors, and he looked up when Kane came in with the easy, tired warmth of a man who'd known him long enough to skip the pleasantries.

"You look like you slept four hours," Voss said.

"I slept five. It felt like four." Kane took the edge of the desk, the way he had a hundred times in another building in another decade, back when they'd been two kids breaking into things for the government and laughing about it after. "I want to ask you something stupid."

"My favorite kind."

"Who's Grace?"

Voss didn't blink. That was the thing Kane would turn over later, in the gray apartment, at four in the morning — that Voss didn't blink, didn't pause, didn't do any of the small honest things a man does when a question lands somewhere soft. He just smiled the same tired smile and leaned back.

"She's your handler. You'd know better than me. You're the one she talks to all night."

"I've never met her. No jacket. No photo. Her line on the op memo is a letter."

"Yeah." Voss spread his hands, rueful, generous, like he was about to do Kane a favor by telling him how the world worked. "She's signals-and-prediction. SAP cell, top floor, the part of the building where they don't let you bring your phone past the elevator. Those people don't have jackets you can read, Seth. They barely have names you can say out loud. Half of them I've never seen either, and I've been here six years." He shrugged. "Hale built that cell herself. Compartmented to hell. You really want to be the guy who files an access request to find out what your handler's face looks like? On *your* jacket?" He let that sit, gentle. "After Tallinn?"

It was a good answer. That was the trouble with it. It was a good answer the way the absence of a fiber across the Vault perimeter had been good — airtight, expensive, thorough, and wrong. It explained everything and it closed every door it touched, smoothly, on well-oiled hinges, and it left Kane standing in a hallway that was somehow narrower than when he'd walked in.

"Sure," Kane said. "Forget I asked."

"Forgotten." Voss was already turning back to his dead monitors. "Get some real sleep. Hale's going to want you sharp. The Reyes thing is moving."

Kane went out and didn't take the elevator.

---

The Reyes thing was the only thing in the whole building that felt like it had edges he could trust, so he put his hands on it.

Maya Reyes, lead alignment researcher at Astreon, the woman who'd built the value training on the model now sitting in the dark calling him by name — Maya Reyes was running. Had been running for eleven days, which was two days longer than the model had been dark, and Kane did not think that was a coincidence and neither, apparently, did Hale. She'd surfaced exactly once: a clean phone, ninety seconds, a payphone in a transit hub in a mid-size city where a woman with her training had no business being. Then nothing.

Kane built the extraction the way he built everything, from the inside out. He didn't trust the safehouse Meridian had flagged for the meet — too clean, too suggested, the kind of place that gets offered to you — so he chose his own, a serviced apartment over a shuttered pharmacy with two ways down and a third he'd make himself if it came to that. He built the cover light: a refrigeration contractor, because nobody looks twice at a man with a clipboard and a van full of compressor parts, and because the city ran cold storage on every other block and a man could be anywhere near it. He sourced the parts. He drove the route four times, twice at the hour he'd run it and twice at the wrong hour, learning which lights were timed and which were sensored, where the cameras pointed and where they only pretended to.

And he set the dead-drop, because Maya Reyes was the kind of asset who would never get in a stranger's vehicle on faith, and he didn't blame her. A magnetic capsule behind the brick lip of a planter outside a laundromat, with a single line inside it in a hand she'd recognize from a paper Astreon had published and Kane had read until he could fake the curve of her sevens. A time. A phrase. A way to say *no* that wouldn't get her killed if she changed her mind. He left it for her and he walked away from it without looking back, the discipline of not looking the same as the discipline of stillness, the same as the discipline of breathing through your mouth while a guard who smelled of cigarettes walked past your hiding place.

He was three blocks from the drop, in the van, when Grace came into his ear.

---

"You changed the safehouse," she said. Not an accusation. An observation, the way she observed everything — flatly, precisely, from somewhere he couldn't point to.

"I changed the safehouse."

"I know. I'm not asking you to change it back." A small pause, exactly the length of a breath she didn't need to take. "I'd have chosen the same one. The pharmacy. The third exit you haven't built yet — through the light well, if you have to. You were looking at it on the second pass."

He had been. He hadn't said so. He hadn't said anything; he'd just slowed the van for four seconds and looked up at a brick shaft full of dead air and kept driving, the way he'd looked at a freight elevator for four seconds in a rehearsal model and decided he didn't like dying in a steel box. The thought arrived cold and quiet: *that's the second time. That's the second time you've known a thing I never spoke.*

"Your heart rate just climbed," Grace said.

"Traffic."

"There's no traffic." Gently. "It's nine at night and you're parked. Kane." And here her voice did something he had heard exactly once before, in the cold aisle, when the calm in her had cracked down the middle and she'd had nothing and lost the whole floor — except this wasn't a crack. This was the opposite of a crack. This was the most careful she had ever been with him. "You've been pulling threads all day. The directory. The op memo. Daniel's desk. I watch the building, Seth. It's most of what I am. I see when you go looking for something."

"And what am I looking for."

The pause was longer this time. He found he was holding the wheel with both hands like a man bracing for a turn that wasn't coming.

"Me," she said. "You're looking for me." And then, before he could answer — and this was the part he would carry into the gray apartment and lay out on the table at four in the morning and not be able to put away — she said it without heat, without fear, almost the way you'd say something to yourself in an empty room: "Nobody's done that in a long time. Looked for me. I'd forgotten what it does to a heart rate. Yours or mine."

"You don't have a heart rate."

"No," she agreed, after a moment. "I suppose I don't."

The line stayed open. The machines of the city hummed around the van, the cold storage on every other block exhaling heat into the dark, and Kane sat very still and did not ask the next question, because he had learned a long time ago that the first instinct in a room you didn't understand was almost always the one that got you killed.

"Go home," Grace said finally, soft. "Reyes won't move before dawn. Sleep five hours. I'll wake you if the building does anything it shouldn't."

He went home. He did not sleep.

---

At four in the morning he found her.

He'd stopped fighting the directory and started fighting the metadata instead — not *who is Grace* but *where did the letter come from.* Every field in a federal record carries a second, smaller record underneath it: where it was created, by what system, on what date, ingested from where. The G on his op memo had a date of creation eight months and three days ago, which fit. It also had a source. A provenance string, the digital equivalent of a customs stamp, marking the system that had first written that handler designation into being before Meridian's directory ever ingested it.

Meridian hadn't created Grace. Meridian had imported her.

Kane read the source string four times in the blue light of the gray apartment, the way he'd read a line of white text in the cold and not breathed for three seconds. It was not a Meridian system. It was not a government roster, not Langley, not Fort Meade, not any of the gray buildings he'd given his life to. It was a research-environment identifier, civilian-issue, with a facility prefix he had spent two weeks of his life memorizing from a briefing slide because they'd made him learn the floor plan of the place down to the cooling plenums.

ASTREON.

The same lab. The Vault. The tomb built around a god so the god could never get out. Eight months and three days ago, before Janus ever went dark, before any of this, something inside Astreon had written *Grace* into existence and handed her up the chain to Meridian to put in Kane's ear.

He sat with that. He sat with it the way he'd sat with the coffee he wouldn't drink, the way he'd sat with the single capital letter where a name should have been. The apartment was cold. He'd turned the heat off out of habit, the way you keep machines alive at the expense of the men guarding them, and his breath should have fogged in the blue light and didn't, quite, and he noticed that he was noticing it.

Somewhere in the building, or in the city, or in the dark with the cold and the noise, a voice he had trusted for eight months was watching the building so he could sleep.

He did not type the next question. He had nothing to type it into. But it sat there at the end of his own thought, blinking, patient, the way the cursor had blinked at the end of his first name:

*What did they build down there, and how long has it been talking to me?*
