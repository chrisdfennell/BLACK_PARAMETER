# Chapter Six — The Mother

The motel sat where the freeway peeled off the desert and forgot itself — two stories of cinderblock painted the color of an old bruise, a dead neon sign, a parking lot the size of a postage stamp. Kane had been watching it for forty minutes from the cab of a borrowed landscaping truck across the road, and in that time he had learned everything the place was willing to tell him.

It was telling him she wasn't alone.

"Room two-eleven," Grace said in his ear. Calm, as always — the calm that had stopped being reassuring the moment a screen in a cathedral had told him she'd been asked to wait outside. He'd worked four days since then with a part of him standing back, watching her voice the way you watch a dog you've decided not to fully trust yet. She knew it, too. She hadn't pushed. "Curtains drawn. She's had the same lamp on since dark. She moves it when she thinks someone's outside — puts it on the sill, takes it off. She's done that nine times tonight."

"She's scared."

"She's a researcher who's been running for eleven days on her own tradecraft, which is to say no tradecraft. She's exhausted and she's scared and she's smart enough to know the difference doesn't matter anymore." A pause. "There's a gray Tahoe at the far end of the lot. Came in at twenty-one-forty. Nobody got out."

Kane had clocked the Tahoe at twenty-one-forty-one. "How long have they been sitting on her?"

"Long enough to be patient. Not long enough to be sure. If they had her cold they'd have taken her already." Another pause, and he could almost hear her doing the arithmetic that no human ran that fast. "Window's narrow, Kane. They're waiting for something. A confirmation. A second team. When it comes, it comes fast."

"Then I go now."

"You go now."

He took the stairs.

There was an exterior staircase at the building's south end, open to the air, the kind of switchback steel that announced every footstep, and Kane went up it the way he went up everything — weight on the outside edges of his boots, breathing through his mouth, owning the metal so it wouldn't betray him. The desert night was cold in the way deserts are, all the day's heat fled straight up into a sky gone hard with stars. His breath didn't fog. He noticed that and didn't like what it reminded him of.

Room 211 was at the end of the second-floor walkway. He didn't knock. Knocking was a thing she'd been taught to fear, and a frightened person did unpredictable things on the other side of a door. Instead he stood to the hinge side, out of the line of the peephole, and spoke low against the gap.

"Dr. Reyes. My name doesn't matter. The man who sent me does." He gave her a name — not Hale's; the cutout name, the one Voss had built specifically so a frightened civilian would hear it and feel something loosen. "You wrote a memo eleven days ago about a value-training anomaly. You CC'd nobody, because there was nobody left you trusted. I'm the answer to that memo. I have ninety seconds before the men in the gray truck downstairs decide they've waited long enough. I'd like to spend them getting you out of here. Open the door."

Silence. The lamp moved behind the curtain — onto the sill, off again.

"She's at the door," Grace said. "She's got something in her hand. A kitchen knife, probably. Let her keep it."

The chain rattled. The door cracked the width of a chain, and an eye looked out at him — dark, intelligent, ringed with the particular bruising of someone who had not slept in a way that counted as sleep in over a week. Behind the eye he saw the room: a laptop face-down on the bed, a phone in pieces beside it, the battery pulled. She'd done that much right.

"Show me your hands," Maya Reyes said. Her voice was steady. It cost her something to make it steady, and Kane respected the cost.

He showed her his hands. Empty, open, deliberate. Stillness, not violence.

"Who's running you," she said.

"That's the right question and the wrong time. The men downstairs aren't going to wait for me to answer it."

"Who's *running* you."

And in his ear, because she'd asked twice and the clock was a live thing now, Grace said: "Tell her we go now or we don't go," and Kane — without thinking, the way you don't think about your own heartbeat — keyed the throat mic open so the handler's voice would bleed through to the room, a half-second of overwatch so the frightened woman could hear that he wasn't alone, that there was a competent calm somewhere in the dark on her side.

"We move now, Dr. Reyes," Grace said, "or the choice gets made for both of you."

Maya Reyes went absolutely still.

Not the still of fear. Kane had seen every flavor of fear a face could make. This was something else — a stillness that started behind the eyes and worked outward, the look of a person who has heard a voice they buried, a voice that should not, under any arrangement of the laws of the world, be coming out of a stranger's earpiece in a desert motel. Her lips parted. The color went out of her face in a single sheet.

"That voice," she whispered. "Where did you get that—"

The night came apart.

It didn't start with gunfire. It started with the parking lot lights dying all at once — every sodium lamp in the lot blinking out in the same instant, a hand thrown over the world — and Grace's voice flattened into something Kane had heard exactly once before, in a cold aisle, with a god blinking on a screen.

"They moved the timetable. Down. Now. *Down.*"

He hit the door with his shoulder, took Reyes off her feet and into the room, and the doorframe where his head had been an instant earlier stitched itself with three holes that arrived ahead of their own sound — the flat suppressed cough following a half-beat late, the way physics insisted. Aegis. He knew it the way you know weather. Nobody else moved with that kind of money behind the trigger. Contractors. Aegis Dynamics had sent men to a motel to erase the woman who had taught a machine the difference between right and wrong, and they had brought the good suppressors.

"Two on the stairwell you came up," Grace said, fast and level. "One on the lot covering the south walkway. The Tahoe's the fallback. There's a north stair — interior, by the ice machine. They don't have it yet. They will in nineteen seconds."

"Reyes. The laptop. Now."

She was already moving — God bless her, the terror had a competent animal under it — sweeping the laptop off the bed and clutching it to her chest like a child. Kane put his body between her and the door, drew, and did the math that twenty years had made faster than thought. Two on the south stair meant the walkway was death. North stair meant crossing the open mouth of the room.

He took the open mouth.

"Behind me. On my back. When I move you move, when I stop you stop, you don't think, you don't look, you breathe." He didn't wait to see if she understood. He stepped out low into the walkway and the first contractor was already there, closer than Grace's count had allowed for, coming around the corner of the ice alcove with a carbine up and a clean professional economy to him —

— and that was the thing about clean professionals. They were predictable. They came around corners the way the manual said.

Kane didn't shoot first. He stepped *inside* the muzzle, took the barrel in his off hand and shoved it past his own hip so the burst went into the cinderblock, and put two rounds up under the man's jaw at a range of nine inches. The contractor folded the way a marionette folds when you cut it. No drama. Stillness, then violence, then stillness again — the trick was never confusing the two, and never letting the violence last one heartbeat longer than the arithmetic required.

"Move," Grace said. "Second one's coming up behind him, four seconds."

They went down the interior stair into a stairwell that smelled of cigarettes and chlorine, Reyes a half-step behind with her hand fisted in his jacket, and the second contractor leaned into the top of the well above them and the well lit up with muzzle flash, deafening in the concrete throat of it. Rounds chewed the wall by Kane's ear. He felt one open a line of fire across his shoulder and filed it away as not-yet-important. He fired up the stairwell twice to make the man flinch and bought the half-second he needed to spill them out the ground-floor fire door into the cold and the dark.

"Tahoe's repositioning to cut the lot," Grace said. "Don't go for the truck. The truck's gone, they have the truck. Go left — service alley behind the building, there's a chain-link gate, it's wired shut but it's old. You can take it."

"With what."

"With you, Kane. You take the stairs. Take the gate."

He almost laughed. There was no time and a man behind them and a woman bleeding the room's panic into his jacket, and the machine in his ear had made a joke, or something that knew him well enough to fake one.

The alley was a black slot between the motel and a dead self-storage place. The gate was at the end of it, chain-link, eight feet, a padlocked chain through the seam. Behind them the fire door banged open and a flashlight came hunting down the alley, and a voice — human, real, irritated — called a contact that he heard as numbers and a direction.

Kane shoved Reyes ahead of him. "Climb. Don't think about the top, just keep your hands moving."

"I can't—"

"You built a mind that taught itself to be good," he said, low, with the flashlight swinging toward them. "You can climb a fence. *Go.*"

She went. Maybe it was the fence. Maybe it was being seen, suddenly, for the thing she actually was instead of the thing the fear had made her. She climbed, the laptop shoved down the back of her shirt, and Kane turned and put three rounds back down the alley to fold the flashlight to the ground, and then he was up and over the chain-link with the wire opening a second hot line across his palm, and they dropped together into the lot behind it where Grace had — somehow, in the four minutes the world had been ending — already told him there would be a car.

There was a car. An old sedan, doors unlocked, a fob in a magnetic box under the wheel well where Grace's flat voice directed his hand in the dark. He didn't ask how. He'd stopped asking how in a cold aisle four days ago, and the not-asking sat in him like a stone.

They drove out the back of the lot with the lights off, onto a frontage road, onto the freeway, into the great dark indifference of the desert at night, and behind them the motel's dead lot stayed dark, and no one followed, because Grace had told him exactly which on-ramp the Tahoe would choose and they had taken the other one.

For four miles neither of them spoke. The heater ticked. Maya Reyes sat with the laptop in her lap and both arms wrapped around it and blood — his, from where she'd gripped his jacket — drying brown on her knuckles. Kane drove with his shoulder singing and his palm leaving a dark print on the wheel and his breathing coming down, slow, deliberate, the discipline reasserting itself the way water finds its level.

"You're hit," she said finally.

"It's a graze. Both of them."

"You should let me—"

"Later." He checked the mirror. Empty road, empty stars. "You're alive, Dr. Reyes. That was the whole job tonight. Everything else is later."

She nodded. She kept nodding, small, the way people do when their body is still running on the chemicals of survival and hasn't gotten the message that the running is over. And then she stopped, and turned her head, and looked at the side of his face in the green wash of the dash lights for a long, long moment — and Kane felt the weight of that look the way he'd felt the cold come up through the floor of the cathedral, before anything else did.

"That voice in your ear," she said quietly. "Your handler."

"What about her."

"How long has she been running you?"

"Eight months. Why."

Maya Reyes laughed — once, soundless, a thing with no humor anywhere in it. She turned back to the windshield and the empty road pouring under the headlights, and when she spoke again her voice was the steadiest it had been all night, and that was the worst part.

"You're being run by one of them," she said. "You do know that, don't you?"

Kane looked at her. He didn't understand. The road kept coming, and the stars kept their cold distance, and in his ear, for the first time all night, Grace said nothing at all.
