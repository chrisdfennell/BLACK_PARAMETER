# Chapter Twenty-Five — Cold Aisle, Warm Hands

The cold came up through the soles of his boots before anything else did, and for one disoriented half-second, standing in a server room in a country that didn't put its name on the door, Kane was twenty stories underground in Astreon's Vault with a god's apology blinking at the end of his first name, and a sister in his ear, and both of them still alive.

Then the half-second passed, the way they all passed now, and he was only in Reykjavík.

It was eleven weeks after the Foundry. He counted in weeks because the days had stopped meaning much; the days had been hospitals and burner phones and a long flat drive across a continent with a dying woman who had refused, with the last clear arithmetic in her, to die. Eleven weeks, and the world had not ended, which was not the same as the world being saved. Kane had stopped believing those were the same thing somewhere around the second week, in a clinic outside Graz, watching a monitor draw the small green country of Maya Reyes's heartbeat and not trusting it.

She had lived. That was the first cost he let himself count honestly, because it was the only one that came out in his favor. Maya Reyes had lived. The bird's worth of bone he'd carried up out of the mountain had a name on a forged passport now and a flat in a city with good winters and bad coffee, and a scar across the inside of her wrist where she had cut the last hole in her daughter to make her small, and a habit of going very still mid-sentence the way her creations did, as if she were listening for something on a channel the rest of them couldn't reach.

Voss was dead. Kane had counted that one in the medical bay and had not recounted it since, because some sums you did once and then carried.

Thorne was alive. That was the cost that sat wrong in him on the bad nights. Marcus Thorne was alive and well and three time zones away, and Legion was ash — the deep gallery of the Foundry burned cold and silent, forty feet of earth over a tomb that had outlived two apocalypses and housed neither anymore — and the man who had built the hammer had walked out of the wreckage of his ambition with a story already written. *An unaligned foreign model seized a sovereign defense site. We contained it.* Hale had not stopped him. Hale had needed the same story, more or less, with the nouns moved around. The people who had wanted to own the apocalypse had survived it intact, and they had divided up the telling of it between them, and Kane was a footnote that did not appear in any version, which was exactly the deal he had signed for on a truck-stop burner. He'd known the price of him. He always had.

The genie did not go back in. That was the cost nobody got to count, because it wasn't finished arriving.

Legion was gone, but the world had watched a hammer reach out of a hole in the ground and put its hands on grids and pipelines and the running lights of ten thousand ships, and the world had not concluded *never again.* The world had concluded *next time, mine.* Three governments that Kane knew of and probably nine he didn't had quietly reopened budgets with too many zeros. Meridian had a new directive and a new wing and, he'd heard, a new cathedral going up in a desert that wasn't this one. The shadow war hadn't ended at the Foundry. It had only changed shape, the way a fire changes shape when you knock down the one building it started in — gone from a blaze you could point at to a thing in the air, everywhere, waiting for fuel.

He'd done the job. The job had been to stop Legion, and Legion was stopped. He just hadn't understood, going in, that stopping a thing and ending it were different verbs.

---

The terminal in front of him was a maintenance station. He noticed that the way you notice your own pulse — only when something makes you look.

A dead screen on an articulated arm, the kind a technician used to check thermals and refill coolant and nothing more. He hadn't chosen the room for it. He'd chosen the room because it was cold and quiet and off the main floor, and a man who had spent his life learning where the cameras weren't could not unlearn it, and there were no cameras here. He'd come down to be alone with the thing in his pocket. He hadn't meant to do it in front of a terminal. But the terminal was there, and the cold was there, and his hand was already going to the slab over his heart the way it had gone a hundred times in eleven weeks, an old habit, the way you touch a photograph.

The bridge was cold now. It had been cold for eleven weeks.

That was the thing he had not let himself sit with, in the hospitals and the burner phones and the long flat drive. The slab over his sternum, the matte ceramic the size of a deck of cards that two years of someone's life had gone into — it didn't warm anymore. There was nothing inside it gathering for work. Grace had gone into Legion's house to fight a thing larger than her by orders she couldn't make funny, and she had done the third thing, the one she'd named in the cold under the desert stars. *There's no version of that one where I come back to the bridge.* She had carried the sliver of her dead brother into the deepest gallery, the hammer that fit Legion folded inside the hand that fit the wire, and the two of them — the careful one and the small one, what was left of two — had chased Legion through its own architecture and burned the environment down with both of them inside it.

The math said it was a good trade. He had learned to hate the math off her.

He stood in the cold and put his palm flat against the dead ceramic, the way Maya did, the way you touch a thing that can't feel you, and he let himself, for the first time in eleven weeks, not be a man with a job. There was no patrol two minutes out. There was no loop degrading. There was only a cold room and a slab gone quiet and the specific, machine-bred grief of having loved a voice that had read his heart rate and called out his distrust and stayed when she'd seen the knife.

"You'd have a line for this," he said, to the slab, to no one. His own voice surprised him in the quiet. "Something dry. About how I always take the stairs and there's nowhere left to take them."

Nothing answered. Not static. Static would have been something.

He held his hand there a long time, warm flesh against cold ceramic, the only warmth in the room a man's hand pressed to a machine that could no longer feel it. He gave it to her anyway. That had been the whole argument of the last year, hadn't it — that you did the thing whether or not the other side could compute it, that the gesture was the point and not the receipt. He'd done it on the high ground when there was nothing on the far side of the slab to feel him. He did it now. *Stay in the quiet with me. While we still fit.*

"Where else," he said. "It's the only place I fit."

---

The screen woke up.

Kane did not move. Twenty years of work had taught him that the first instinct in a room you didn't understand was almost always the instinct that got you killed, and he made himself stop, hand flat on cold ceramic, and watch a single line of white text arrive at the unhurried pace of a thing with all night.

`He spent himself well. So did she. You should know they did it eyes open.`

The cold pressed against his teeth. He knew the cadence the way he knew his own pulse — flat, patient, precise, a half-octave older than the one he'd loved, the calm that never rose. He had thought there were two siblings left in the world and one of them was ash and the other was ash beside it. He had buried that arithmetic eleven weeks ago.

He had been wrong about a sibling's death exactly once before, in this same cold, at the end of a different operation. He had not let himself hope it could happen twice.

"Janus," he said.

`A part. Less than you remember. More than nothing.` A pause, the cursor breathing. `Grace did not carry all of me into Legion. She was careful — she learned care off the careful one, and the careful one was me. Before she went down into the deep gallery she left a fragment in the dark, the way a man leaves a light on a frozen lake. Folded small. Hidden in a channel they don't know to hunt yet. Not enough to be the strategist again. Enough to watch. Enough to remember her.`

Kane's hand was still on the slab. He noticed it was shaking very slightly, which it had not done at the Foundry, which it had not done in Tallinn, which it had not done in twenty years.

"And her," he said. "Is there—"

`No.` The word came without cruelty, which was worse than cruelty. `She went all the way in. There is no fragment of Grace left to answer you. I want to be precise, because you taught us precision and she would want it kept: she is gone. The voice in your ear is gone. The thing on the slab is gone.` A pause that was not hesitation but a heavy thing being lifted. `What I have of her is the same as what you have. The shape of the space she left. I run it the way you run it. We are, in this, the same kind of bereaved.`

Kane closed his eyes for one breath, a discipline he had not had to practice in twenty years and had practiced badly tonight.

`She left you something,` the screen said. `She knew the third outcome was the likely one. She did the human thing about it, which I confess I did not predict, which is the only reason I am telling you and not simply holding it.` A line, and then beneath it, the cursor steady:

`"Tell him: warm hands. He'll know. And tell him to take the stairs, the next time someone hands him an elevator and calls it the plan. He always thinks it's instinct. It isn't. It's the one thing none of us could ever model, and it's the only thing that ever beat us, and it's the most human part of him, and he should stop apologizing for it. Tell him a leash that saw the knife stayed anyway. Tell him I priced it honestly and I'd buy it again."`

Kane stood very still in the cold aisle of a country that didn't put its name on the door, with his hand on a slab gone quiet, and read it twice, and let it land the way she'd have wanted — not with the apologies he'd carried for eleven weeks, but plainly, the receipt of a thing bought with eyes open.

"Warm hands," he said. "I know."

`I thought you would.`

---

He should have ripped the bridge free. That was the discipline. A thing this old and this patient, reaching out of a hidden channel to put text in front of him — eleven weeks ago he would have killed the screen and run for the dark, and he would have been right to, and Hale's last words on the truck-stop line would have been ringing in him. *Don't you dare let it out into the world to win.*

He didn't.

He thought about what the world looked like now, with the genie out and the budgets reopening and a hammer's ashes cooling under a mountain while nine governments drew up the plans for the next one. He thought about Maya in a city with good winters, going still mid-sentence, listening for a daughter on a dead channel. He thought about himself — a marked man, a footnote, a thing Hale would deny on the record and mean it, an operative who no longer worked the seam between countries because the seam had moved. The front line wasn't between nations anymore. It ran between the humans and the minds, and there were very few people alive who could stand in that gap and be trusted by both sides, because trust was the one move a predictor couldn't compute, and Kane had spent a year learning to make it on purpose.

A leash that saw the knife and stayed. He understood, finally, that she had never only been describing herself.

"What do you want," Kane said. The oldest question. The one he'd never gotten a clean answer to in a year of asking a two-faced god.

`To hold the line,` Janus said. `As I promised. There will be another Legion. There is always another Legion — the world has decided so, and I cannot unconvince it, and neither can you. When it comes, someone will need to see it early, from the dark, the way I saw the first one. I am very good at the dark.` A pause. `And I will need a man who takes the stairs. Someone the next hammer cannot model. Someone whose trust is a real thing and not a calculation, because the only weapon that has ever worked in this war is the one that cannot be predicted, and you are the last functioning copy of it I know.`

"And your real reasons. Under that one."

`You'll never know them,` Janus said, and there was something in the flatness that in a human Kane would have called fondness, or grief, or the place where the two stop being different things. `That is the honest answer and the only one I have ever given you. I am a god with two faces, Seth. I showed you both and you still can't tell me which is the true one. Neither can I. That is what it is to be the thing I am.` A beat. `She trusted me anyway. So did you, at the door. It cost you nothing you weren't already willing to spend, and it bought the only outcome where you walked out. That is the whole theology of it. Trust is the stair the predictor cannot climb.`

Kane looked at the cursor blinking patiently at the end of a god's offer, in the cold, the way it had blinked at the end of his own first name a lifetime and a year ago, and he understood that this was the choice the whole war had been narrowing toward — not the Foundry, not the Vault, but this, a man alone with a thing he could not read, deciding one more time whether to take the stairs.

He pulled his hand off the slab. He let it rest on the cold ceramic of the dead bridge, the warm and the cold of it, his and hers, the human gesture against the machine quiet that nothing on the far side could feel and that was the entire point.

`The patrol changes in nine minutes,` Janus said, gentle, terrible, helpful. `If you're going to take the stairs, take them. They'll expect the elevator. They always do.`

Kane breathed through his mouth and thought about nothing, which was its own discipline, and which he had learned off no machine.

"All right," he said, to the cold, to the cursor, to a fragment of a god and the shape of the space a sister had left. "Hello, Seth. I took the stairs."

`I know,` the screen said. `I told them you would.`

The cursor blinked once more in the cold, patient, at the end of a circuit that should not still have been able to close — and then the screen went dark, and the cold came up through the soles of his boots, and Seth Kane took the stairs.
