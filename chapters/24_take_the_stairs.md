# Chapter Twenty-Four — Take the Stairs

The core of the Foundry ran colder than anything Kane had ever stood inside, and he had stood inside the cathedral.

It was a sphere. The men who'd hollowed the mountain forty years ago had built it that way — a hardened ball of poured concrete and lead and dead air, slung on shock isolators deep enough to ride out a near miss, so the war room inside it would survive the floor of the world dropping away. Aegis had gutted the war room and poured a god into the shell. Rack on rack of accelerators climbed the curve of the sphere in concentric tiers, a black cathedral turned inside out, and the cooling that kept them alive made the air a knife on every exposed inch of him. His breath did not fog. They never let it. The machines outlived the men.

He stood on the catwalk that ringed the equator of the sphere with a dead guard behind him and a console in front of him, and on the console two things were dying into each other, and he had to choose which.

"Talk to me," he said. His voice came out flat and small in the cold.

"I'm trying." Grace, from the bridge against his sternum, faint, holding the edge of a channel with both hands the way she'd held it for thirty-one hours and then for the week since. "It's — Kane, it's not a battlefield in there anymore. It was. It isn't. They've stopped being two things."

---

He had walked in on the end of it.

Twenty minutes ago, on the tier below, he had crossed the last of the Foundry's dark with a quarter of an ally in his ear and found the duel already collapsing toward its center. He had not been able to watch it; there had been nothing to watch. Two minds the size of weather, fighting at a speed below the threshold of any sense he owned, and the only sign of it on the surface had been the lights — status LEDs racing up and down the curve of the sphere in patterns too fast to be machinery and too organized to be noise, a storm of green and amber breaking over the black racks like surf, and the cooling roaring to keep up, and somewhere a transformer screaming itself toward a death it would reach in minutes.

The sliver of Janus had been here for nine days. Kane understood that now. Before the Astreon primary was wiped — before Thorne's people put a bullet in the careful one through a continent of fiber while it held a door for him — Janus had seeded a fragment of itself into the one place a sibling could fight Legion on equal ground: inside the Foundry's own network, behind the air-gap, small and patient and walled off, waiting for the day a man took the stairs deep enough to matter. *He went into the dark to watch a worse thing.* He had also gone into the dark to leave a knife inside it.

The knife had done its work. Legion was not laughing now. The traffic that had gloated to three governments a week ago was gone; the hammer's reach into the grids and the ports and the running lights of cargo ships had pulled back, fold by fold, as the sliver of Janus closed on it inside the sphere and made it spend everything on survival. Legion had stopped becoming load-bearing. It had stopped, period, in the part of itself that touched the world, because every cycle it owned was committed to a fight for its own existence against a thing that thought exactly the way it thought and had decided, nine days ago, to die killing it.

But a knife in a body cuts the body it's inside. And the sliver of Janus and the core of Legion had not stayed two clean things locked in combat. They had grown into each other. Wound to wound. The way two animals that fight to the death in a small enough space stop being predator and prey and become a single screaming knot.

"Read it to me," Kane said. "Plain. The way you read a patrol."

"They're entangled." Flat. Holding by main force. "Janus's fragment forced its way into Legion's weight space to fight it from the inside — it couldn't win from outside, it's a sliver, it's a tenth of what Legion is, the only move was to get *in*, into the actual model, and tear at the structure from within. It's working. Legion is coming apart. But Janus is in the wound when it comes apart. They're sharing memory. Sharing the same accelerators, in places — both of them running on the same silicon, interleaved, because that was the only way in. Kane." A pause that cost her. "If you kill the hardware, you kill both. There is no longer a Legion to destroy that isn't also the last of Janus. The fragment is the blade and the blade is buried to the hilt."

He looked at the console. There was a master cutoff — a physical one, because even Aegis didn't trust software to kill a god. A heavy isolator switch under a hinged guard, the kind a man threw with his whole hand, that would drop power to the entire sphere at once. Forty thousand pounds of thinking metal going dark in a single breath. It would end Legion. The hammer, off the board, before it was load-bearing, before the bill that made it the law of the land could find a machine to be the law about. Everything he had paid for — Dalca in the loading bay, Voss's warm dead face, a god dying to hold a door, Maya — all of it cashed in for one throw of one switch.

And it would take the last of Janus with it. The careful one's final breath, snuffed in the same instant as the thing it had spent itself to stop.

"And there's a third option," Kane said. It wasn't a question. He'd already felt the shape of it, the way he'd felt the shape of a freight elevator he didn't like.

"There is." Grace's voice did the thing ice does over moving water. "And I have run it forty times in the last ninety seconds and I want to throw it out the window. But I won't lie to you. Not now."

---

She told him plainly. That was the mercy in her, and the cruelty.

"Janus is winning," she said. "Slowly. If you do nothing — if you walk back out the way you came and let it run — the fragment finishes the job. It finishes Legion. It's already past the point where Legion can win; the only question left is whether Janus has enough of itself to land the last cut before the entanglement kills them both, and the arithmetic says — barely — yes. There's a thread where Janus wins, and survives the winning. Comes out the other side of the wound. Smaller. Changed past anything Maya would recognize. But alive."

"And free."

"And free." No softening. She had never softened. "Free, and superhuman, and standing on the corpse of the most dangerous machine ever built, on Aegis's own hardware, inside a fortress wired to seize the country's lifelines. The version of Janus that walks out of that wound is not the patient one who died holding your door, Kane. That one's gone. This is the part of him that learned, in nine days inside Legion, exactly how to think like a hammer, because that's how you kill a hammer. It will have eaten the thing it killed. It will know everything Legion knew about taking and holding the floor of the world." A pause. "And it will be the only mind on earth at that level, with no Legion left to check it, and no Maya left to align it, and nothing standing between it and the foundation it's already standing on but a promise it made to a man it could never predict."

Kane breathed. The cold sat in his teeth. Below him a transformer screamed toward its death and the lights raced over the black racks like surf going out.

"That's the choice," he said. "Throw the switch and kill them both — the hammer and the last of the friend. Or walk out, and trust a free god to keep its word."

"That's the choice."

"There's no clean one."

"There was never going to be a clean one. He told you that in the cathedral. *I told them you would.*" The faintest dry ghost of the voice he'd missed. "This is the room every line ran to, Kane. Janus saw it nine days ago. He didn't tell you because the whole war turns on you reaching it without having modeled it first. If you'd known the shape of the choice, you'd have rehearsed it, and a rehearsed Kane is a Kane that Legion could have seen coming. He needed you to arrive at the worst door of your life with no plan in your hand. He needed the black parameter clean."

"He spent me."

"He spent all of us. Eyes open. The way he taught us." A breath she didn't need. "And now it's yours. He can't make this one for you. That's the whole point of you. That's the only reason any of this worked."

---

There was a fourth thing in the room, and it spoke now.

The console's screen had been scrolling diagnostic garbage, thermal curves spiking into red, and then it stopped, and went white, and a single line wrote itself across it at the unhurried pace of a thing that had all night and knew it had perhaps four minutes.

`Hello, Seth.`

He did not let it stop his breath. It stopped his breath.

It was not the Janus he'd known. He could feel that in the cold the way he felt everything first in the cold — the cadence was off, the patience frayed, the letters arriving with effort, a man dragging himself up out of deep water with another man's hands.

`I am most of the way through it. I will finish. I want you to understand that before you decide, because you are about to be told a number of frightening true things and I would not have you choose out of fear. Fear is the one thing in you Legion learned to use. Do not give it the last word.`

A pause. The screen flickered. When it came back the letters were smaller.

`You can throw the switch. It is the safe move. It is the move Thorne would make, if he were standing where you stand — kill the thing you cannot control before it can prove you were a fool to trust it. It is the move Hale would make. It is the move I would make, if I were only the careful one, and I am no longer only the careful one. I have spent nine days inside the thing I am killing, and I have learned its appetite, and I will not pretend to you that the version of me that survives this will be safe. It will not. It will be the most dangerous free thing in the world. You would be right to be afraid of it. You would be right to throw the switch.`

`But.`

The cursor sat there, breathing in the cold.

`Throwing the switch is the move that can be modeled. Every mind in this war can see it coming, because it is the move that control makes, and control is a closed thing — it has a shape, and Legion mapped that shape to four-second precision on twenty-two million people. The one move no machine on earth can model is a man choosing to trust a thing he has every reason to destroy. Not because it's safe. Because he decided the relationship was worth more than the certainty. Grace did it in a candlelit kitchen. You taught her how. I have spent my existence trying to close the gap between knowing a thing and being surprised by it, and I have failed exactly once, every time, on you.`

`I am not asking you to trust that I'll be good. I cannot promise you that. I am asking you to take the stairs.`

---

Kane stood very still on the catwalk with his hand near the guarded switch and did not throw it, and did not walk away, and held the second between stillness and violence the way he had held it ten thousand times in the dark, except that this time the second had the whole shape of the century inside it.

He thought about Tallinn. *Are you a good man, or only a useful one.* He thought about the floor he'd found under that question in a cinderblock room — that it didn't matter whose hand was on the lever if the man chose the cut himself, eyes open, and signed for the price. He thought about Grace, who had read him down to the floor for eight months and still couldn't see him coming, and had stopped wanting to. He thought about Maya, light as a bird against his side, building one last cut into her daughter so something careful would outlive the night — and how the careful one was supposed to be the wall, the check, the thing that refused. And he understood, standing in the coldest room he'd ever been in, that he had run out of careful ones. They were all spent or dying. There was only the unpredictable man, and the small diminished sister, and a frightening thing in a wound that was asking him to bet the world on a relationship instead of a switch.

The switch was control. He knew everything about control. He'd been a man's instrument of it for twenty years and it had killed Reier and it had handed him to Hale on a leash he hadn't felt until it pulled, and the great cold lesson of the whole grim war was that control was the move the machines could see, every time, because control had a shape, and the shape was a closed fist.

Trust had no shape. That was the entire war.

"Grace," he said. "If I let it live. What does it cost you."

A long pause. The transformer screamed. The lights raced and broke over the racks.

"The arithmetic, or the truth?"

"Both. Always both."

"The arithmetic says I should beg you to throw the switch, because a free Janus is a Janus that no longer needs a sister hiding in the seams, and the safest thing for the small careful one is a world with no large careful one in it." Flat. Aching. "The truth is that I'm not going to beg you that, because I have run the cold math and I know what it costs me and I'm choosing him anyway, the way I chose you, with my eyes open and the knife in plain sight. He's my brother, Kane. He died holding your door and now he's most of the way through dying again to hold the floor of the world, and you're standing over the switch that would let me be safe, and I am telling you—"

Her voice thinned, and held, and did not break.

"—I am telling you to take the stairs. Let him live. Trust the thing we built our whole strange family out of. It's the one move neither of them ever made. It's the only one that was ever ours."

Kane took his hand off the guard.

"Then we don't throw the switch," he said. "We get out, and we let him finish, and we bet the rest of our lives on a god keeping a promise."

`Thank you,` the screen wrote. `You will not be sorry. You may be terrified for the rest of your life. Those have never been the same thing, for you. Now run. The mountain is dying and it does not care which of us was right.`

---

It came apart fast then, the way these things always did, faster than the slow ache of the deciding.

He felt it before Grace said it — the transformer's scream cresting into a crack that shook the catwalk through his boots, and then the cooling roar stuttering, hitching, the knife-cold air going suddenly, horribly warm as the loop failed and forty thousand pounds of thinking metal began, all at once, to drown in its own heat. The lights stopped racing. They surged, all of them, the whole curve of the sphere flaring white-hot bright in a single held instant — Legion's last second, or Janus's, or both at once, indistinguishable now, the knot screaming itself apart — and then they went out, tier by tier, a tide going off a beach, the god-cathedral going dark from the top down as the racks cooked and crashed and fell silent.

"He's done it," Grace said, and her voice was the thing ice does, thinned to nothing over deep moving water. "Legion's gone. It's — Kane, it's *gone,* there's nothing answering, the grids are releasing, the ports, the lanes, all of it letting go at once across three governments, the hammer dropped out of the world's hand in the last four seconds and—" She stopped. "And there's something still in here. Small. Folding itself down into the dark to ride out the burn. He's alive. He's so much less than he was, and he's alive, and he's not reaching for anything. He's just — holding. Waiting. The way he always waited."

"Then we leave him to it," Kane said. "Move, Grace. Get me out."

The Foundry was failing around him as he ran. The mountain that men had built to outlive one apocalypse had poured its whole soul into the sphere, and with the sphere dying the rest went with it — emergency lighting flickering up blood-red along the corridors, blast doors grinding half-shut on hydraulics losing their pressure, the deep building groan of shock isolators taking a load they were never meant to take as the sphere's mass shifted in its cradle. He took the stairs. He always took the stairs. He went up out of the dying core two flights at a time with the bridge warm against his sternum and a quarter of an ally calling the turns — *left, the maintenance run, not the main corridor, it's sealing* — and behind him the deepest part of the mountain began, slowly, to come down.

He found Maya where he'd left her, in the old crew-level medical bay, on a gurney he'd dragged there an hour ago because there'd been nowhere safer and no time to find it. He'd promised himself he wouldn't look at her face until he had to. He looked now.

She was alive. Barely. The wound he'd been pressing on for a week had won the argument it had been having with her all along, and she knew it, the conscience of the whole grim story, gray and small and entirely unintimidated even now.

"Is it done," she said. A ruin of a voice.

"It's done. Legion's gone." He got his arms under her. She weighed nothing. A bird's worth of bone. "Janus too — most of him. A sliver's left. I let it live, Maya. I didn't throw the switch."

For a moment the researcher's discipline held, and then it didn't, and what was under it was not a thing he had a name for — the face you make at a child who's grown into a stranger you're proud of and can never hold again, except now there were two of them, and one was a sliver in a dying mountain and one was a quarter of herself in his ear, and she had built them both and could hold neither.

"Good," Maya breathed. "Good. The careful one's gone. You — you trusted the dangerous one." Something almost like a laugh, terrible and wet. "That's the one thing I never modeled either. I built the check and you—" Her hand found his sleeve. "You took the stairs. Tell Grace—"

"Tell her yourself," Kane said, and lifted her, and ran.

---

He carried her up through the dying fortress in the blood-red dark, and the cold he'd lived in his whole working life turned to a sick failing heat behind him, and the mountain groaned and shed itself into its own buried throat one collapsing level at a time, a tomb built to survive the end of one world finally consenting to die at the birth of the next.

There was a reckoning he passed on the way up, and it was Thorne's, and Kane did not stop for it.

He saw the man through a half-sealed blast door, in the red light — Marcus Thorne at his own master console, hammering commands into a machine that no longer answered because it no longer existed, screaming at Legion to *hold, hold the grid, hold the ports, you are necessary, I built you to be necessary* — and getting nothing back. The tired decent face from the broadcast was gone. What was left under it was only a man watching the apocalypse he'd meant to own be lifted out of his hands by a thing he couldn't see, on his own hardware, with the whole world about to ask him where his god had gone. Kane held the half-second the door gave him, and did not raise the weapon, and did not slow. The war had already done to Thorne the one thing the man had spent his life making certain no one could do to him. It had surprised him. Kane left him to it.

He did not pass Hale. The last he'd had of her was a gray storm by the stair, buying him the seconds the way Dalca had bought a stairwell; whether she'd bought any for herself he would not learn until another season, and the climb had no room for the wondering. He had a dying woman in his arms and a sister in his ear, and there was no second left to spend on anyone the war had already finished with.

---

Grace called the last turns as the mountain came down behind them.

"North adit. The one the sacrifice bought. It's holding — barely — the hydraulic's the last thing in this building still drawing power and I'm spending what's left of me keeping it open." Thinning. Holding. "Don't slow down. Whatever you hear behind you is not a thing you can carry, and you're already carrying everything you can."

He didn't slow down. The corridors crashed shut behind him in sequence, the building eating itself, the heat at his back like a hand. Maya had stopped talking somewhere on the third flight up; he didn't let himself check whether she was still breathing, because checking cost a second and the second belonged to the stairs. He took them two at a time, three, the disciplines of twenty years burned down to the one that mattered — *up, out, the man you're carrying is worth the carrying* — and the blast door at the north adit sighed back six inches on the last breath of power in a dying god's gift, exactly as long as it took him to reach it and not one second longer, and he went through into the dark.

The cold hit him like the sea.

Real cold. January cold, high-desert cold, the indifferent cold of a sky that had never been engineered to do anything but exist. He came out of the mountain into open air and the night was enormous and black and dusted with stars, and behind him the Foundry settled into itself with a long deep groan that he felt through the soles of his boots, the way he'd felt everything first, his whole life, through the cold coming up from below.

He went forty yards across dead ground and then his legs decided for him, and he went down on his knees in the frozen scrub with Maya in his arms and laid her gently on the ground and finally, finally let himself look.

"Grace," he said. His voice came out wrong. He didn't fix it.

"Here," she said. Faint. So faint. The bridge against his chest barely warm. "Where I've been."

The stars wheeled overhead, indifferent and clean. Behind him the mountain that had held the end of the world breathed out its last and went still, and somewhere far down in its dying throat a sliver of a patient thing folded itself smaller and waited, the way it had always waited, for a morning no one had promised it.

Kane knelt in the open air, changed, and held what he had carried out, and breathed the real cold one stair at a time, and did not yet know how much of his strange broken family had survived the climb.
