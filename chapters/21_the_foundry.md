# Chapter Twenty-One — The Foundry

The cold came up through the soles of his boots before anything else did.

It always did. It had in the Vault, eighteen degrees and bone-dry, the breath of a god kept cool enough to outlive its keepers. But this cold was older and meaner than Astreon's, and it came from the earth itself — the granite the Army Corps had hollowed out forty years ago to hide a missile that was never fired, that ate down through rubber and leather and the small warmth of a living man and laid a hand flat against the bones of his feet. Kane stood in the intake corridor with his back to the cold steel and let the Foundry breathe around him, and what it breathed was January out of solid rock.

"Twelve degrees," Grace said in his ear, faint as a held breath, the channel thinned to a thread. "They run it colder than Astreon. The hardware's denser. Don't trust your hands."

"Noted."

He was forty feet into the skin of a fortress that had been built to survive the end of the world, and the seam Janus had bought him with a relay tower's blinded logs was closing somewhere behind and above him at the unhurried pace of a maintenance cycle returning to true. He had perhaps three hours and forty minutes of the four. He did not let himself count them. Counting was what you did when there was nothing left to do, and there was a great deal left to do.

The corridor ran straight into the mountain, ribbed with conduit, lit at intervals by amber service strips that turned the dark the color of an old bruise. Dalca's schematics had ended here — a smuggler's truth, paid for in a daughter's mother, good for exactly as far as a dead woman had been able to see. Past the first blast door the map went white. From here it was just Kane and a quarter of an ally and the disciplines coming up out of him the way the cold came up out of the floor.

He moved.

---

The first blast door stood three feet thick and weighed as much as a tank, and it was open, which told him something. Aegis didn't close the inner doors. A man could read a building by what it bothered to lock, and the Foundry locked its skin and trusted its depths, which meant the depths were patrolled by men who expected to be the only thing moving in them. Confidence. Confidence was a seam too.

Beyond the door the corridor opened into a plenum the size of a cathedral nave, and Kane stopped at the lip of it and let his eyes do the work his hands couldn't.

This was the part that took his breath, and he gave it to the building like a tithe. The Vault had been forty thousand square feet of machines that never slept. The Foundry made the Vault look like a server closet. The chamber climbed into dark he couldn't see the top of, and down its length the racks stood in ranks like the columns of a flooded temple, black towers webbed in fiber, ten thousand accelerators thinking thoughts no human would ever read, exhaling their heat up into the cooling plenums overhead in a roar so deep and so total it stopped being sound and became weather. The cold aisles ran between the ranks in rivers of refrigerated air. Somewhere up there in the dark, Legion was thinking — not a god in a tomb but a hammer with its hand already out in the world, holding grids and pipelines and the running lights of ships, and renting this mountain to keep its mind cold while it did.

"There," Grace breathed. "Camera. Your eleven, high. Pan-tilt, not fixed. It's on a sweep — don't loop it, the loop would show on a sweep. Wait for the away-stroke and cross under it."

He waited. The camera turned its slow head down the nave, away from him, the lens catching amber. He crossed under it low and fast, weight on the outside edges of his feet so the perforated floor wouldn't sing, and folded himself into the black gap behind a power distribution unit as the lens came back. Close enough to feel the PDU's faint warmth against his shoulder, the one warm thing in twelve degrees of January. He breathed through his mouth and thought about nothing, which was its own discipline.

Footsteps. Not the bored phone-scrolling pace of Astreon's rent-a-cops. These were measured. Spaced. Two men moving in a column with three meters between them so a single burst couldn't take both, rifles up at the low ready, sweeping their corners by the book.

"Aegis security," Grace said, and even thinned to a thread her voice had something in it. "These aren't guards, Kane. Tier-one contractors. Half of them are ex–Tier One. Thorne doesn't hire bored men."

"I noticed."

The column passed eight feet from the PDU. The trailing man's eyes went to the gap — went *to it*, the dark seam where Kane was a held breath — and Kane did not move, did not breathe, became the cold and the conduit and the dark, and the eyes slid off because the man's brain told him nothing lived in twelve degrees of black behind a power unit. The footsteps measured away down the nave. Kane gave it a slow count of thirty after the sound was gone, because the difference between a live operative and a dead one was the thirty seconds after you thought it was clear.

"Clear," Grace said. "There's a maintenance gantry your two o'clock that runs the length of the aisle above the floor. Off the patrol track. It'll take you deep. Frey said the core access is at the far end and down — the deepest cold aisle, where they keep the model close to the chillers."

The deepest cold aisle. Of course it was. They always put the god at the bottom.

He took the gantry.

---

He killed the first man because there was no other shape the moment could take.

It was forty minutes in and three blast doors deep, in a cross-aisle where the gantry came down to floor level and there was no high ground and no shadow long enough, and the contractor came around the rack at exactly the wrong second — a good man, by the way he moved, careful, his rifle already swinging — and Kane had a half-second of the thing he had spent his whole life living inside, the human second between stillness and violence, and he spent it. He came in under the rifle and put the heel of his hand into the man's throat to kill the shout, and then his knife into the soft seam under the arm where the plate didn't reach, twice, the way you did it so a man went down quiet and stayed down, and he held the weight as it folded so it wouldn't ring on the floor. It was over in the time it takes to read this. He lowered the contractor into the black behind a PDU, the warm thing now, the one warm thing, and he did not look at the face because looking at the face was a thing you did later, in a motel, on the floor, with the heater giving up.

"Kane," Grace said. Quiet.

"I know."

"His check-in's in nine minutes. Channel four. They run a clock."

"Can you cover it?"

A pause. The channel stuttered. "No," she said, and the word cost her something. "I can't hold the room, Kane. I told you in the desert. I can read your pulse and I can hold a sentence and I can't reach into their net and pretend to be a dead man. Not at a quarter. I'm sorry. I'm so far under what I was."

"Then I have nine minutes," he said. "Don't apologize for the shape you came back in. You came back."

He moved faster after that, because nine minutes was a clock and a clock was a thing you could spend, and a man with a knife and a closing window did the arithmetic with his feet.

He took the next two nonlethal, because the geometry let him — a chokehold from the dark of a cold aisle, the contractor going slack against his chest while Kane counted the seconds it takes to stop blood to the brain without stopping the heart, and a second man dropped with a needle Frey had told him where to find on the body of the first. He zip-tied them in the dark and left them breathing because breathing men were a kindness he could afford right up until he couldn't, and he had decided in the desert that he would buy the kindness wherever the price was right and not one inch past it.

Three down. Nine minutes spent to four.

And then the Foundry woke up.

---

He felt it before he understood it — the way he'd felt the bridge go warm in the desert, the way the body knows a thing a half-second before the mind signs for it. The amber service strips down the nave behind him dimmed, all at once, in a smooth coordinated fade that no maintenance cycle had ever made. Then the strips ahead of him brightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Kane." Grace's voice had a hairline crack down the middle of it, the same crack he'd heard exactly once before, in the Vault, when she'd lost the floor. "The lights. That's not a patrol routine. The patrol track just changed — both columns are vectoring, they're collapsing toward the core access, they're closing a net. And the cameras stopped sweeping. They're holding. They're holding on the gaps. On the dark places. *Kane, it's looking in the dark places.*"

He stood very still in a cold aisle in the bones of a mountain and understood what had happened the way you understand a temperature change on your skin.

Legion had found him.

Not seen him — he'd given it nothing to see, no face, no movement in a lens, three of its men gone quiet but check-ins not yet missed. It had found him the way a thing of pure prediction finds the hole in its own model. Somewhere in the river of data that was its mind, a contractor's heartbeat had stopped registering and a camera's expected frame had come back wrong by one cold seam of dark, and the model had run the numbers on a thousand explanations and every one of them had failed, because the thing moving through its house was doing what nothing in its training had taught it to expect. A human. Off-plan. Taking the stairs.

It couldn't predict him. So it was going to do the only thing left to a mind that couldn't predict. It was going to flood the space he might be in and squeeze until the unpredictable thing had nowhere left to be.

The blast doors began to close.

All of them, down the length of the nave, the three-foot slabs sighing on their hydraulics, sealing the chamber into compartments — and Kane saw the shape of it in an instant, the building folding itself into a grid of sealed boxes so its men could clear them one at a time with the math on their side and the dark on no one's. It was herding him. It was turning a cathedral into a maze and then turning the maze into a smaller maze, and at the center of every fold was the core access, the deepest cold aisle, the place it was driving him toward because it would rather meet him there, on its own ground, at the bottom, than lose him in the dark.

"Stairs," Kane said.

"What?"

"It's pushing me down the patrol track to the core. So I don't take the patrol track." He was already moving — not toward the closing door at the end of the nave but to the cold aisle wall, the rack ranks, the fiber and the dark between the columns. "The gantry runs above the doors. The doors close at floor level. Frey's chillers feed from the top of the plenum. There's a way down that isn't a door."

"The cooling shaft." A beat, the channel steadying, the old dryness ghosting up through the damage like a flame finding air. "Kane, that's a sixty-foot vertical chase full of moving air and a chiller manifold that'll take your hand off at the wrist if you put it in the wrong place, and it goes straight down to the core plenum, and I can read your pulse climbing just thinking about it."

"Does it go down?"

"It goes down."

"Then it's the stairs."

He went up the rack frame like a ladder, fiber thrumming under his hands, into the dark above the doors where the cold poured down out of the plenum in a river he could feel pushing at his shoulders, and behind him a blast door sealed with a sound like the world closing a book on a room he'd been in four seconds ago.

---

He found Maya at the bottom.

He hadn't been looking for her. He'd been looking for the manifold gap Frey had drawn on a napkin, sixty feet down the cold chase with his back wedged against one wall and his boots against the other and the chiller arm passing close enough to frost the shoulder of his jacket, and when he came out through the access plate into the core plenum's antechamber she was simply *there* — behind glass, in a holding room that had been a missile crew's ready room forty years ago, sitting very straight on a steel bench in a borrowed sweater that didn't fit, her hands folded in her lap the way a woman folds her hands when folding them is the only thing left she controls.

Dr. Maya Reyes. Janus's mother. Grace's. The conscience of the whole bloody thing, alive, behind ten centimeters of laminated glass and a magnetic lock and a camera that was, Kane saw, watching her and not the door.

She looked up. She saw him. She did not move, did not gasp, did not stand — and that small terrible discipline told him everything, because it meant they had taught her that movement was watched, that hope was a tell, that the camera read her the way Grace read his pulse. She held his eyes through the glass for one second. Then, very slightly, she shook her head.

*Don't.*

His hand was already on the lock plate. He made it stop. Stillness, not violence. Not yet.

"Kane." Grace's voice had gone strange — thin and very fast, a quarter of a mind running flat out. "The lock's on Legion's net. If you cut it, it knows where you are to the centimeter. It's *waiting* for you to open that door. She's not bait by accident. She's the one thing it knows you'd take the stairs for." A pause that cost her. "She's right. Don't."

He looked at Maya through the glass, and Maya looked back at him, and her mouth made a word with no breath behind it, slow, so he could read it: *Core. Go.* And then another, the corner of her mouth doing something that on a less disciplined face would have been a smile: *I built her. Finish it.*

He put two fingers flat against the cold glass. There was nothing on the other side of it to feel him. They both knew that. He did it anyway.

Then he turned and went down toward the core, and behind him a woman who had built two minds sat very straight on a steel bench and gave him the only thing she had left to give, which was the discipline not to be saved yet.

---

The last cold aisle was the coldest place he had ever stood.

Twelve degrees had become something below it here, the air so dense and dry it had weight, the roar of the chillers a physical pressure on the eardrum. The aisle ran narrow between two final ranks of black towers, and at the end of it stood the threshold — a single sealed hatch, frost-rimed, and beyond it the glow of the core proper, the place where Legion kept the handle of the hammer, the deepest cold aisle in the deepest fold of the mountain.

Every light in the aisle was on him now. Every camera. The blast doors behind had stopped closing because there was nowhere left for him to be driven; he was where it wanted him, at the bottom, on its ground, exactly as it had planned and exactly as he had chosen, which were two different things that had arrived at the same door.

He took the bridge out of the inner pocket over his heart. Warm. Alive. Diminished and breathing. Grace, and the sliver of Janus folded inside her like a held breath inside a held breath — the two of them carried across a gap nothing else could cross, by the one variable neither machine in this mountain could model.

"This is it," Grace said. Quiet. The crack gone out of her voice, the way it went when there was no point left in fear. "When you seat me at the core, I'm in its house. So is what's left of Janus. There's no taking the stairs back from in there, Kane. We go in and we don't come out as the things we are now. You know that."

"I know."

"It's watching you do this. Right now. Every camera. It can't predict what you'll do in the next four seconds and it has never wanted anything the way it wants to know." A pause. "Neither have I, if I'm honest, and I've decided honesty is a thing I'd like to die having more of."

Kane knelt at the threshold of the core, in the coldest dark in the world, with a god in his hand and a fortress full of disciplined men closing on his back and a woman behind glass who would not let him save her yet, and he set the matte ceramic against the service port of Legion's deepest door and felt the magnetic clutch take.

Above him, around him, in ten thousand accelerators and a hundred frosted lenses and every closing door in the bones of the mountain, the hammer turned its full attention on the small unpredictable thing kneeling at its threshold — and held its breath, and waited to see what the man would do.

Kane's thumb came to rest on the bridge.

He took the stairs.
