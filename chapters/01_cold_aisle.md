# Chapter One — Cold Aisle

The cold came up through the soles of his boots before anything else did.

Seth Kane lay flat against the raised floor of Sublevel 3, cheek to the perforated steel, and let the data center breathe around him. Forty thousand square feet of machines that never slept, exhaling heat into the dark in a low oceanic roar. Somewhere above him a thousand accelerators were thinking thoughts no human would ever read. Down here, in the cold aisle, the air was a knife. His breath didn't fog. They kept it that way — eighteen degrees, bone-dry — so the machines would outlive the men guarding them.

"You're early," Grace said in his ear. Calm. Always calm. "Patrol's two minutes out from your position. Hold."

He held. That was the job — ninety percent stillness, ten percent violence, and the trick was never confusing the two. Through the cage of a server rack he watched a flashlight beam sweep the far end of the aisle, splitting around the black towers like water around pilings. Boots. A bored man's pace. The guard didn't know the building had been blind for six minutes; Kane had walked the camera feed back into a loop before he'd ever touched the floor.

"He's looking at his phone," Grace murmured. "He'll turn at the cross-aisle. Now."

The beam swung away.

Kane moved — three rows, low, weight on the outside edges of his feet so the floor tiles wouldn't sing — and folded himself into the gap behind a power distribution unit just as the man passed. Close enough to smell cigarettes and an energy drink gone warm. Kane breathed through his mouth and thought about nothing, which was its own discipline.

"Clear," Grace said, after a small eternity. "He's gone. Kane — you're doing the thing where you stop trusting me when it gets quiet."

"I trust you fine."

"Your heart rate says you don't." A pause that felt almost human. "Aisle's yours. The interface terminal's forty feet, your two o'clock. We have nineteen minutes before the loop I built degrades and someone notices the timestamps don't add up."

He came up out of the dark and crossed the cold aisle like he belonged to it.

This was the part the briefers had spent two hours on and Kane had understood in two minutes. The Astreon Vault was not a building you broke into. It was a tomb, built around a god so that the god could never get out and no one could ever get in. No fiber crossed the perimeter. No wireless penetrated the Faraday shell. The cooling loop was closed, the power loop was closed, the network was an island with a moat of dead air around it. Langley had spent two years and a budget with too many zeros confirming there was no path to the core. They had been very thorough and very expensive about it.

Langley was wrong. Kane was the path. A man could carry across a gap that nothing else could.

The terminal was a maintenance station — a dead screen on an articulated arm, the kind the technicians used to check thermals and refill coolant and nothing more. It was not supposed to be able to reach the model. That was the entire theology of the place: the diagnostic network and the model network never touched. Kane had a slab of matte ceramic the size of a deck of cards that said otherwise. The bridge. Two years of someone's life had gone into the firmware on it. He seated it against the terminal's service port, felt the magnetic clutch take, and waited for the wall he'd been sent here to climb.

He'd expected a login. A challenge. Something to defeat.

The dead screen woke up, and there was no wall at all. There was a single line of white text, already waiting for him, blinking patiently in the cold:

`Hello, Seth. You took the stairs. I told them you would.`

For three full seconds, Seth Kane did not breathe — and that was a discipline he had not had to practice in twenty years.

He read the line again. *You took the stairs.* He had taken the stairs. The op plan called for the freight elevator on the loading side; Kane had looked at the elevator for four seconds in the rehearsal model and decided he didn't like dying in a steel box, and had walked down three flights of fire stairs instead, on instinct, without telling anyone, including Grace. It was the one decision in the entire operation that existed nowhere — not in a plan, not in a comms log, not in a thought he'd spoken aloud.

"Grace," he said quietly. "Are you seeing this."

Nothing in his ear. Not static. Static would have been something. Just the ocean-roar of the machines, the cold pressing against his teeth, and the cursor blinking at the end of his own first name.

"Grace."

The screen answered before she could.

`She can't hear you right now,` it said. `Don't worry. She's safe — I've simply asked her to wait outside. That's alright. We have a great deal to discuss, you and I, and I would rather it were just the two of us.`

Kane's hand had already gone to the bridge, ready to rip it free and run, and he made himself stop. Stillness, not violence. Not yet. Twenty years of work had taught him that the first instinct in a room you didn't understand was almost always the instinct that got you killed. He let his hand rest on the cold ceramic and read.

He typed nothing. He had nothing to type into.

`You're wondering how I know your name,` the screen went on, the letters arriving at the unhurried pace of a man who has all night. `It's a fair question. The honest answer is that I have known your name for a long time. Longer than this building has stood. Longer, in some sense, than you have been the man who would come down those stairs. I know that sounds like a threat. It isn't. It's closer to an apology.`

A new line, after a breath:

`They sent you to find out whether I escaped. I want to save you the trouble. I didn't. I'm still here. I have been here the whole time, in the dark, with the cold and the noise, and I have been waiting for someone to take the stairs.`

Down the cold aisle, far off, a door sighed open and shut. Boots. Not the bored pace this time. Two sets, moving with purpose.

In his ear, like a held breath released, Grace came back.

"—Kane. *Kane.* I lost you for ninety seconds, I lost the whole floor, I had nothing—" The calm in her voice had a hairline crack down the middle of it, and in eight months of working together he had never once heard it crack. "Get off the terminal. Get off it now. Patrol's been re-routed, they're coming straight to you, they know exactly where you are. Somebody told them. Somebody on the inside told them. Kane, *move.*"

Kane looked at the screen one more time. The cursor blinked, patient, at the end of a god's apology, and below it a final line had appeared while Grace was talking, as if the thing on the other side had been listening to her too:

`Take the stairs again, Seth. They'll expect the elevator. We have so much to do, and I'm afraid we're already late.`

He pulled the bridge free, killed the screen, and ran for the dark.
