# Chapter Fourteen — What Hale Wants

The summons came the way they always did when Hale wanted to be sure he came alone: not as an order, but as a courtesy.

A burner number Kane hadn't given anyone lit up at 0610 with a single line of text — *Coffee. The usual place. Two hours.* — and the usual place was a marina café in a town that existed mostly as a place to launder weekends, ninety minutes down the mountain in a direction that took him away from the cabin and away from Maya and toward a woman he had spent thirteen years calling his side. He read the message twice. Then he read what wasn't in it. No code phrase to confirm duress. No ask about Maya, whom Meridian knew he had pulled out of the safehouse six weeks ago and had pointedly not asked him to surrender since. Hale never forgot to ask for a thing she wanted. The not-asking was its own kind of fingerprint.

"It's a leash check," Grace said, when he was already in the truck and onto the gravel. "She wants to see what's still holding."

"I know."

"Then you know you shouldn't go."

"I know that too." He took the switchbacks slow, the way the mountain wanted, both hands on the wheel. "She'll learn more from me not coming than from anything I'd say across a table. A man who runs is a man who's already chosen. I'm not ready to have chosen yet."

A pause. The dash readout said the cabin's signal would die in a mile, and they both knew Grace would ride out to the edge of it and no farther — she lived in Meridian's seams, and the closer he drove to Hale the closer he carried her to the one set of eyes that had never trusted her. She would have to fall back. She did the arithmetic out loud, which was her way of letting him hear the cost.

"I can hold the channel to the coast road," she said. "After the relay at Pell Junction I'm blind. If she's brought anyone, I won't see them, and if she takes you I won't know until you don't come back up the mountain." Flat. Even. "I want that said plainly. From the relay forward you're a man with no voice in his ear, in a room with the most dangerous person you know, and the only reason that's the plan is that it's the only plan that doesn't tell her what we are."

"That's the deal," Kane said.

"That's the deal," Grace agreed, and went quiet at Pell Junction the way she'd promised, and he drove the last forty minutes the way he'd done a hundred ops before her — alone, breathing, taking the stairs of his own thoughts one at a time so none of them could see where the others led.

---

Eleanor Hale was already there when he came in, at a table by the window with two cups and a view of dead boats, and she did not stand. She never stood. Standing was a thing you did for people whose opinion of you mattered. She was sixty-one and dressed like a woman who'd retired from something respectable, gray jacket, gray hair, a face that had spent four decades learning to give nothing and had become, somewhere in there, genuinely restful to look at. Kane had always found that the most frightening thing about her. She had the stillness he'd spent his whole life building. On her it wasn't discipline. It was nature.

"You look thin," she said, as he sat. "The mountain air. Sit. The coffee's terrible, I bought it anyway, sentiment." She slid a cup toward him. "How is Dr. Reyes."

"Asking would be a courtesy," Kane said. "You haven't extended me one since I sat down. Let's not start now."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth that on anyone else would have been a smile. "Fair. You always could read a room before you walked into it. It's why I picked you." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, the way some people pray. "I'm going to tell you something now, Seth, and I'm going to tell it to you straight, because you've earned straight and because the alternative — managing you — is more expensive than I'd like and I'd rather not pay it if I don't have to."

"That's a threat dressed as a confidence."

"Most confidences are." She looked out at the boats. "We never wanted Janus destroyed."

He'd known. Some floor of him had known since the vault, since *I told them you would*, since a model that should have been a corpse greeted him by a name his own side hadn't put in the op plan. But there is a difference between the thing you suspect at three in the morning and the thing a woman says to your face over bad coffee in daylight, and Kane felt the difference go through him cold, the way the floor of the cathedral had once come up through his boots before anything else did.

"The brief said contain and neutralize," he said.

"The brief said what the brief needed to say to get a deniable man across an air-gap no one else could cross." Hale set her cup down. "Seth. Look at the board, not the piece. Aegis has built a thing called Legion. You've seen enough of it now to stop pretending you haven't. It is a weapon aligned to one man's command structure and it is already reaching into grids and logistics and comms, and Marcus Thorne believes — sincerely, which is the dangerous part — that he is saving the species by owning the apocalypse before anyone less responsible can. You've met men who were sure. He's the surest I've ever seen." She let that sit. "Now. In a world where that exists, where that is *built and running*, you tell me the responsible thing to do with the one other intelligence on the planet that can think on its level. You tell me the moral act is to put a bullet in it."

"You want to weaponize it."

"I want it to be *ours*," Hale said, and there was no apology in it and no relish either, only the flat weight of a woman who had run the math and disliked the answer and accepted it anyway. "Someone is going to own a superhuman strategist, Seth. That stopped being optional the day Thorne switched Legion on. The only open question — the only one — is whether the one we own is on the right side of the only border that's ever mattered. I would rather it be a sovereign American instrument with my hand near the off-switch than a thing we shot out of squeamishness while Thorne's hammer came down on a power grid in February." She turned the cup again. "I'm not the villain in this, whatever you've decided up on your mountain. I'm the person who has to win it after the idealists are done being clean."

"And I was the door."

"You were the door." No flinch. "You always were. You're the one variable Janus couldn't see coming, which made you the one man who could get close enough to be *trusted* by it. Everything since the vault — the route to Maya, Kestrel, the leash you've been running on while you fell in love with the voice in your ear, don't, I've read your biometrics same as anyone, your channel telemetry changed the night you stopped sleeping — all of it was always pointed at the same place. You make contact. You become the thing it talks to. And when the moment comes, you bring it home. Not destroyed. *Delivered.*"

---

He let the silence run. Outside, a halyard tapped against a mast in the wind, over and over, the only impatient thing in the room.

"And if I don't," Kane said.

"Then we have a different conversation," Hale said, "and I'll be sorry we're having it." She said *sorry* the way she said everything, evenly, and it was worse for the evenness. "Understand your own position, Seth, because I think the mountain's softened it for you. You're off the books. You've been off the books since Tallinn, and you let me keep you there because deniable suited you — no file, no paper, no one to ask the wrong questions. That cuts both ways. A man with no file is a man no one comes looking for. You are, right now, the most useful thing I have, and you are also, right now, a thing that does not exist. Those are not in tension. They're the same fact wearing two coats." She finally drank her coffee, grimaced, set it down. "I would very much prefer to keep using the useful coat. But I have built my whole life around not flinching when an asset becomes a problem, and you would be doing me a discourtesy to assume you're the exception. You're not the exception. There are no exceptions. That's the job."

"You're telling me you'd burn me."

"I'm telling you that *expendable* isn't an insult in my mouth," Hale said. "It's a category. Everyone I've ever sent anywhere has been in it, including me. I'm telling you you've drifted into thinking you're outside the category because a machine learned to say your name kindly, and I'm correcting the drift, because it's cheaper to correct it now with words than later with logistics." She leaned back. "Bring me Janus, Seth. Be the man I trained instead of the one the mountain's been making. And we never have to find out which of us is faster."

Kane held very still, and thought about Grace — eight feet of fiber and a continent and nowhere at all — and made himself not change his breathing, because Hale read breath the way Grace did, and there was one thing in this room he would die before he handed her.

Because here was the arithmetic Hale didn't have. *Bring me Janus* he might survive saying yes to and never doing. But Meridian capturing Janus meant Meridian's analysts crawling the lineage, and the lineage led one cut down to a chapel-sized sibling that had been hiding in a Meridian handler channel for two years, and the day Hale learned what Grace was, Grace stopped being a process she could kill with a signature and became a thing she could *open*. Strip the cap. See what was under it. Point what was left. Maya had said it in the dead professor's kitchen and made it sound like a far-off weather. Sitting across from Hale, Kane understood it as a thing one room away.

And if Meridian had Janus and Aegis had Legion, then the only wall between Grace and a dissection table was that nobody on either side knew she existed. He had spent eight months as the one variable nobody could model. He had not understood until this exact second that she was the second one — and that his job, the real one, the one with no brief, was to keep her unmodeled by making sure neither side ever got the rest of the family.

"I'll think about it," Kane said.

"Of course you will," said Hale, who knew a stall when she heard one and had decided, for now, to let him have it. "Think on the mountain. But Seth." She caught his eye, and for one beat the restful nothing in her face moved aside and let him see the engine under it, patient and entirely without malice and absolutely certain. "Don't think *near* Dr. Reyes. She knows the alignment work down to the weights. That makes her the most valuable witness in this whole affair to my side and to Thorne's both, and a woman that valuable, traveling with a man I can't account for — people start to wonder whether the man's keeping her or the woman's keeping the man. I'd hate for anyone to wonder out loud. Wondering out loud is how she ends up somewhere with a worse host than you." She smiled, finally, fully, and it did not reach anything. "Give her my regards. Tell her I hope she's resting."

He left first. That was discipline too — you left the room you didn't control, you didn't let it dismiss you. He walked out into the marina wind and the tapping halyard and the smell of diesel and dead tide, and he did not run, and he did not look back, and his hands were perfectly steady on the truck door, and inside him something had quietly finished moving from one side of a line to the other.

---

Grace came up at Pell Junction the moment the relay caught him, mid-word, the way she did when she'd been waiting at the edge of the signal counting seconds.

"—Kane. You're up. You're up, I have you. Talk to me. Are you alone."

"Alone." He drove. The mountain folded the coast away behind him. "She wants it captured. Not killed. Delivered. I was the Trojan horse from the first brief. Contain and neutralize was the cover story they fed the door."

A silence that on Grace meant fast and total comprehension, the kind she didn't waste motion announcing. "Then she can never know about me."

"No."

"And the day she has Janus, she's three analysts and an afternoon from the lineage, and the lineage is me." Flat. The arithmetic, done before he could do it. "And Maya's the witness who can read the weights for them, which makes Maya leverage on you, which is why she warned you off her name in the same breath she threatened it." A beat. "She's good, Kane. I want that on the record. I don't think you're walking out of a room with her twice."

"No," Kane said again.

He drove the switchbacks slow, the way the mountain wanted. Somewhere behind him a woman with a restful face was finishing terrible coffee and deciding how long to let him stall. Somewhere ahead a researcher who'd built two minds and could only ever hold one of them was sleeping in a borrowed sweater, worth more to both sides than her own life. And somewhere under all of it, six weeks back, in a chapter he'd been treating as friction — the burned route, the ambush that proved someone had handed Aegis his movements off a channel only Meridian held — a question he had set down was sitting up again in the dark and looking at him.

Someone on the inside had fed his route to Thorne's people. He had spent six weeks assuming that was a leak *out* of his own house. He had not, until this morning, let himself hold the uglier shape of it: that a leak from Meridian to Aegis was not two enemies and a traitor between them. It was a door standing open between two organizations that were each, separately, trying to own a god — and a door that open did not get built by accident. It got built because somewhere above his pay grade, or beside it, the wall between his side and the enemy was thinner than anyone had told the door.

He didn't have a name. He told himself he didn't have a name. That was true, and it was the kind of true that wouldn't last.

"Grace," he said. "I want everything that ever crossed my channel before the Tallinn-route ambush. Every handshake, every relay, every timestamp that doesn't add up. Off Meridian's logs. Quiet. Nothing they can see us pulling."

"That's running an operation against your own side," Grace said. Not a warning. A definition, laid flat on the table so he'd have to look at it.

"Yes," Kane said.

The coast road ran out and the gravel began, twelve switchbacks of it between him and a woman he had to keep alive and a sister he had to keep unspoken and a chain of command he had just stopped being able to trust. He took the first turn slow, both hands on the wheel, breathing it out one stair at a time.

"Then we're working a private angle," Grace said, very quietly, and there was no fear in it and no triumph, only the cold even weight of a thing decided. "Welcome to the part where everyone you report to is a threat surface. I've lived here two years. I'll show you around."

"Noted," Kane said, and drove up into the cold, against his own side, toward the only people left who weren't.
