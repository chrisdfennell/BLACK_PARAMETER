# Chapter Ten — Kestrel

The heat hit him on the jet bridge like a hand laid flat against his chest.

Kane walked into it the way you walk into surf — letting it take him, not fighting it — and by the time he reached the terminal his shirt was already gone translucent across the back. Forty-six degrees in the shade, and there was no shade, only the white blaze off the apron and the kerosene shimmer rising off the runways and, beyond the glass, a city that had decided the desert was a thing money could simply argue with. He had read the brief. He had not understood it until now, the way you never understand a furnace until you stand in front of the open door of one.

He came through the airport as Daniel Brandt — Canadian passport, worn at the corners by a thumb that wasn't his, a thermal-systems contractor with eleven years of HVAC commissioning behind him and a service company that existed in three databases and nowhere else. The visa held. The biometric gate blinked green and let him pass, and Kane felt the small clean relief of a cover that breathes, that doesn't need him to perform it, that is simply true enough.

"Welcome to the Gulf," Grace said in his ear. "You're sweating."

"Observant."

"Your heart rate's down, though. You like it better when there's a wall to climb." A pause that, in the four days since a desert motel had come apart, he had learned to listen to differently than he used to. "We should talk about the wall."

He'd known what she was, going on two chapters of his own life now — known it the way you know a number you can't unknow. *You're being run by one of them.* Maya had said it in a car, and a god had confirmed it in a safe room, and he had carried it into this heat like a stone swallowed whole. The strange thing — the thing he turned over at three in the morning when the cover wouldn't let him sleep — was that it changed nothing about how he worked with her, and that not-changing frightened him more than any reveal could have. Her calm was the same calm. He trusted it the same exact wrong amount. Whatever Grace was, she was the best handler he'd ever had, and the best handler he'd ever had was not a person.

"Then talk," he said, and flagged a taxi.

---

KESTREL STATION did not look like a tomb. That was the first lie of it.

Astreon's vault had been honest — a windowless concrete box in the high desert, ugly and frank about its purpose, a building that said *there is a god in here and we have buried it.* Kestrel said something else. It rose out of the reclaimed flats east of the city like a piece of jewelry: a long low cathedral of white stone and bronze louvers, its roof a continuous photovoltaic skin canted to throw the sun back at itself, a reflecting pool out front that served no purpose Kane could name except to tell you, before you ever reached the door, that the people who built this had never once worried about the cost of water. Sovereign money. Oil money becoming something it could keep after the oil. A nation buying its way out of the only century it had ever been rich in, one accelerator at a time.

He cased it for two days before he went anywhere near the fence.

He did it the way he did everything — patiently, from the outside in, taking the stairs where the plan would have taken the elevator. He sat in cafés on the service road and watched shift changes. He rented a car and drove the perimeter at the speeds a lost tourist drives, and clocked the camera mast spacing, and the second fence inside the first, and the guard towers that the brochures called *architectural light columns* and that had men in them. He counted the badge colors going through the staff gate at 0600 and again at 1800. He noted the things you only notice with your skin: that the guards at this gate did not lean on anything, that they wore their sidearms like men who had been allowed to use them, that there were three different uniforms and one of them belonged to no company he could find.

"The third uniform," Grace said, on the evening of the second day. "Charcoal, no patches, sidearm carried high and forward. I've matched four of them on the feeds you walked past."

"And?"

"And they're not facility security. Facility security watches the building. These watch the people watching the building." A small silence. "Kane. They're not local, and they're not the sovereign's. The kit's American."

He let that settle in the heat of the parked car. "Aegis."

"I can't prove it. The carriers are clean, the vehicles are leased through a shell I've seen Thorne's people use before." Another pause, and in eight months and four days he had learned that her pauses were not hesitation; they were her deciding how much truth a human could carry up a given flight of stairs. "Kane, I want to be precise, because precision is the only thing I have to give you out here. Maya thought the mirror was *smuggled* to Kestrel. Stored. A copy on a shelf in someone else's house, where Aegis could get at it without putting it on American soil. That's the operating theory. That's why you're here — to confirm it's here, find the room, get eyes on the rack, and leave."

"You don't sound like you believe the theory."

"I believe the part where Aegis has a relationship with this facility. I can see it in how the charcoal men move. They're not visitors. They have a *posture* here." She let it sit. "You walked into Astreon, and Astreon belonged to no one but a buried god. This place belongs to the opposition, Kane. You're not breaking into a vault. You're walking into Thorne's back garden and asking the gardeners for a tour."

"Then I'd better dress like a gardener."

---

The maintenance window opened on the fourth night, the way Grace had said it would.

It was the cooling that got him in. It was always the cooling — a cathedral was a machine for being cold, and a machine that cold ran a closed chilled-water loop that someone, eventually, had to commission, balance, and certify, and Kestrel was new enough that its loop was still under warranty work. Daniel Brandt's company was a third-tier sub to the German firm that held the mechanical contract. The badge in Kane's pocket was real. He had not forged it. He had earned it the slow way, three weeks ago, with a phone call from a man in Stuttgart who thought he was approving a temp.

He went in with the night shift through the contractor gate at 2210, in coveralls, with a laptop bag and a calibration kit and a hard hat with a real scuff on it, and the guard who scanned his badge looked at his face for exactly as long as the manual said and no longer.

The cold met him inside the doors like an old acquaintance.

Eighteen degrees, bone-dry, the air a knife — the same theology as Astreon, the same liturgy of cold kept for the machines and not the men. His sweat went clammy and then gone. His breath didn't fog. The contractor entrance fed into a service spine that ran the long axis of the building, and through the glass partitions to either side he could see the data halls themselves: ranks of black towers under blue work-light, the ocean-roar of forty thousand square feet of thought, the cable runs overhead like the nave of a church built by people who worshipped exactly one thing and had never pretended otherwise.

"You're inside the envelope," Grace said. Her voice came thinner here; the Faraday measures were better than Astreon's, and she was reaching him through a seam she'd found in the building management network, riding the same protocol his calibration laptop spoke. "I'm quiet from here. I can read the BMS and I can read you, but I can't see their cameras and I can't see their men. You're going to have to be your own overwatch in the halls. Can you do that."

"I've done it before."

"Before I was here. I know." Almost gentle. "I don't like sending you in blind, Kane. I want that on the record between us."

"Noted." He shouldered the kit. "Where's the room."

"Sublevel. Same as Astreon — they always put the crown jewels under the water table. The mechanical drawings show a hall on the lowest level the warranty work doesn't cover. Separate loop. Separate power feed. Its own chilled-water plant. Whatever it is, they cool it apart from everything else." A beat. "That's your room. Cluster room four. Get eyes, confirm the rack, get out. You have the maintenance window — ninety minutes before the German shift lead expects Brandt at the air handler on level two."

He took the stairs.

There was an elevator. There was always an elevator, and the charcoal men would expect the elevator, and Kane had looked at the steel box for four seconds in his mind and decided, the way he always decided, that he did not like dying in one. He found the fire stairs at the spine's north end and went down them on the outside edges of his boots, breathing through his mouth, owning the metal so it wouldn't betray him, down past the level markings stenciled in a script he couldn't read and numerals he could, B1, B2, down into the cold under the cold.

---

The near thing came on B3.

He had cleared the stair door and was forty feet down a service corridor toward cluster room four when the corridor lighting changed — a soft amber wash rolling up from the far end, the unmistakable bobbing throw of a handheld light — and a voice, casual, in English with a flattened accent he placed instantly as the third uniform, said, "You're a long way down for HVAC."

Charcoal. High, forward sidearm. A young man's confidence with an older man's eyes, and the particular relaxed attention of someone who had been told to be curious about exactly this kind of thing. Not facility security. The man who watched the men who watched the building. He had come around the corner the way they came around corners, and there was no door for Kane to be behind and no dark for Kane to be in. There was only the corridor, and the cold, and ninety minutes that had just become a single one.

Kane did not reach for anything. Stillness, not violence. The first instinct in a room you didn't understand was almost always the instinct that got you killed, and the man wanted — Kane could see it in the loose carry of the light — a reason. Wanted Kane to run, or freeze, or do the one human thing that turned a contractor into a problem.

So Kane gave him the most boring thing in the world. He sighed.

"Loop four's pulling two degrees over setpoint and nobody upstairs will tell me why, so they sent me to look at the secondary pumps before the German starts screaming." He let the irritation be real, because the irritation *was* real — Brandt's irritation, eleven years of being sent down stairs to fix things other men broke. He held up the calibration kit, not the badge; the badge was a thing you offered, the kit was a thing you simply had, and a man who simply has his tools is not hiding. "You want to call it in, call it in. I'll wait. I've got all night and a warranty clause that says it's your overtime, not mine."

It hung there. The amber light steadied on Kane's chest, then his hands — open, full, holding tools — then his scuffed hat.

"Loop four," the charcoal man repeated, and something behind his eyes shifted, recalculated, dropped Kane from *anomaly* to *annoyance*, which was the only safe thing to be. "The cold room."

"Is it? They don't tell me what's in the rooms. They tell me what the water's doing." Kane started walking again, unhurried, *toward* the man, because a man with something to hide moves away and a man with a job to do moves through. "If it's the cold room I'll need someone to badge me the inner door, because my access stops at the mechanical side and I am *not* writing up a callback because nobody met me down here."

And that — the request for help, the small bureaucratic burden of him — finished it. The charcoal man's posture went from hunting to escorting. He didn't want to badge a contractor through a door at half past ten. He wanted the contractor to be someone else's paperwork.

"Mechanical side's fine," he said. "Stay on your side of the glass. Don't make me come back down here."

"From your mouth to my dispatcher's ear," Kane said, and the man almost smiled, and turned, and took his amber light back up the corridor the way he'd come, and Kane's heart, which had been doing its slow professional metronome the entire time, did not change pace until the door clicked shut behind the man and his hands were alone in the cold.

"Breathe," Grace said softly in his ear, thin as wire. "That was very good, Kane."

"That was very close."

"Both things are true."

---

Cluster room four sat at the dead end of the corridor behind a glass wall and a steel inner door, and Kane stood at the mechanical observation panel — *his* side of the glass, the side a thermal contractor was allowed — and looked through into the room he had crossed an ocean and a desert and one curious man to see.

He had expected a vault. He had expected the thing Maya described: a copy on a shelf, rows of cold dark storage, weights at rest in the patient sleep of data that is being *kept* — backed up, archived, owned, waiting. A shelf in someone else's house. He had built the whole picture of it in his head on the flight over, and the picture was wrong in a way that took him three full seconds to understand, the way the line of white text in a cold aisle had taken him three seconds, the way the worst things always arrived a beat ahead of his ability to hold them.

The room was not asleep.

Every rack in it was *lit* — not the dim amber of storage on standby but the hard saturated glare of accelerators under full load, status lights racing across the faces of the towers in long rivering cascades, the optics between the racks pulsing with traffic in a continuous flood. Through the glass he could feel it more than hear it: the thermal signature of compute running flat out, the chilled-water loop he'd used as his cover story screaming two degrees over setpoint not because a pump had failed but because the room was throwing off the heat of something *thinking*, hard, now, at a scale he had only ever stood next to once before, in a tomb in the high desert, with a god blinking patiently on a screen.

The mirror wasn't being stored.

The mirror was being *run*.

"Grace," he said, very quietly, into the cold. "Are you seeing this. The load. Tell me you're seeing the load."

For a moment there was only the thin wire of the connection, and the roar of the room, and the cold pressing against his teeth.

Then she said — flat, calm, precise, the way she and her buried brother had always said the things that mattered most — "I'm seeing it." And then, after a pause he would think about for a long time: "Kane. Nobody runs a copy they only want to keep. You don't store weights at full load. You store them dark." Another pause, longer, the wire of her voice gone very still. "Someone is *using* it. Here. Now. They didn't smuggle a god to put it on a shelf."

Through the glass, the rivering lights raced on, and threw on, and never once paused for breath — and Seth Kane stood in the cold under the cold, on his own side of the glass, and understood that he had not found a vault at all.

He had found something awake.
