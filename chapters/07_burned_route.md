# Chapter Seven — Burned Route

The contractors had known the room number.

Kane kept coming back to that, the way a tongue keeps finding a broken tooth. Not the motel. Not the town. The room. They had stacked on the door of Unit 14 with breaching charges already shaped and zip-cuffs already out, and they had done it eleven minutes before the time he had given Meridian for the handoff, because the eleven minutes were the part that mattered — the part where Maya was supposed to be alone and he was supposed to be on the road. Somebody had wanted her taken clean and him gone. Somebody who had read the plan.

He sat now in a Meridian safe room that smelled of new carpet and old coffee, forearms on a steel table, and let the cold of the morning bleed out of him slowly. He had not slept. There was a flat ache behind his eyes that he recognized as the particular fatigue of a man who has spent six hours doing arithmetic he doesn't want the answer to.

The arithmetic was simple. The route from the safehouse to the exfil point had existed in four places. Grace had built it. Voss had logged it. Hale had signed it. And Kane had walked it. Four. That was the whole list. He kept making the list longer in his head — a courier, a watch officer, a janitor with a badge — and every time, it collapsed back to four, the way a stone rolls back into the same low place no matter how you set it.

"You're doing it again," Grace said in his ear. Quiet. The safe room was supposed to be swept and shielded, but Grace got in anyway, the way she always did, through some seam in the Faraday that nobody had thought to caulk. "The list."

"There's a list to do."

"There is." A pause that, in eight months, he had learned to read as her choosing her words. "I'm on it too, Kane. Four names. I'm one of them. I'd rather you didn't pretend otherwise — it'll make you worse at this, not better."

He said nothing. He appreciated that she'd said it. He distrusted that he appreciated it.

---

Hale came in without knocking, which was a thing she did to people, and set a folder on the table that she had no intention of opening. Eleanor Hale was gray in the way of someone who had decided, decades ago, that color was a luxury that got operators killed. She sat across from him and folded her hands and looked at him as though he were a budget line that had run over.

"Tell me about the stairs," she said.

He almost laughed. "Wrong building."

"Tell me what you actually did at that motel that wasn't in the plan. Because something wasn't, and that's the only reason you're sitting here and not in a ditch."

So he told her. He told her how he'd put Maya in 14 and then sat in the truck across the lot watching the road, because a safehouse you've been given is a safehouse someone else can give too, and he'd never liked a door he hadn't chosen. How the two SUVs had come in dark, no headlights, rolling on the engine brake, and how that — the discipline of it, the *quiet* of it — had told him everything before the first boot hit the gravel. These weren't local muscle. This was tradecraft. He'd gone in through the bathroom window and taken Maya out the same way while the stack was still dressing the front door, and they had spent the rest of the night being two people in a country that suddenly had no safe place in it.

Hale listened without moving. When he finished she said, "You think it's one of mine."

"I think the route was burned. I think it was burned from inside. I don't think it walks itself out of the building."

"No." She said it flatly, conceding nothing and everything. "It doesn't." She turned the folder a quarter inch on the table, an alignment, a tell of someone who needed her hands to have a job. "I'm going to tell you how this goes, Kane, because you've been off the leash long enough to forget. You report to me. Not to her—" a flick of her eyes upward, at the air, at Grace, whom she had never trusted and never said why "—and not to your gut. There is a leak. I will find it. You will keep extracting product from Reyes and you will not, under any circumstances, start a one-man inquisition that tips the leak that we know there's a leak. Are we clear."

"We're clear."

"We are not clear. You're agreeing with me, which is how I know we're not." She stood. At the door she paused, and for a moment the gray cracked into something almost tired. "I lost two men in Tallinn cleaning up after a route that got burned. I am aware of the cost of this. Don't tell me about it."

She left. Kane sat with the folder she hadn't opened and understood that she had told him exactly nothing and exactly everything, which was the only language Hale spoke.

---

Voss found him an hour later in the equipment bay, and the relief of it — a friendly face — was so immediate and so complete that Kane noted it and set it aside, the way you note a current that's pulling you somewhere you didn't choose to go.

"Jesus, Seth." Voss gripped his shoulder, hard, and Kane let him. They had bled together in three countries. Daniel Voss was the warm one, the one who remembered your kid's name and your bad knee and the bar in Riga, and in a unit built out of gray people that was worth more than rank. "I heard about the motel. I've been pulling the comms logs all night trying to figure out how. I can't — Seth, I can't find it. The route never went out on any channel I can see. It's like it leaked out of nowhere."

"It didn't leak out of nowhere."

"No. I know. I know." Voss dragged a hand down his face. "I keep thinking — okay, the staging server, right? Anyone with a token could've pulled the movement order off the staging server. I'm building the access list now. It's long, Seth. It's longer than you'd want." He said it apologetically, the way you'd hand a friend bad news you wished you didn't have. "I'll find them. I'll find every name that touched it. Just — give me till tonight."

Kane looked at his old partner and found nothing in his face but worry, and worry was the right thing to find, and that was the problem. He didn't know yet that he would replay this conversation a hundred times in the months to come — the *staging server*, the *access list that's longer than you'd want*, the gentle widening of the suspect pool from four to forty. He only felt, faint as a draft under a door, a wrongness he couldn't name and so dismissed.

"Tonight," Kane said. "Thanks, Danny."

"Always." Voss squeezed his shoulder again and was gone, and Kane stood in the bay listening to the absence of him and disliking himself.

---

They were keeping Maya Reyes in a windowless room two floors down, in protective custody, which was a phrase that meant *prison you're grateful for*. She sat with her back to the wall and her knees drawn up, and she had the eyes of someone who had not stopped doing math either — but her math was older, and went further, and frightened her in a register he didn't have access to yet.

"They came for me," she said, before he'd sat. "Not for you. You noticed that."

"I noticed."

"Good. That's good. You're not stupid, which means we can do this faster." She studied him. She was small and exhausted and entirely unintimidated, a woman who had spent her career arguing with something far more powerful than Kane would ever be. "You want to know who they were."

"I know who they were. I want you to confirm it."

"Aegis Dynamics." She said the name and watched it land. "Marcus Thorne's people. Defense AI — they do battlefield decision systems, autonomy stacks, the things that let a colonel push less paper and kill more people. That's the cover story. The cover story is true, which is what makes it good cover." She tilted her head. "You've met men like Thorne. Patriots. The kind who've decided that the only way to keep the wrong people from owning the apocalypse is to own it first, very responsibly, with excellent intentions and a flag on the wall."

"And he wants Janus."

"He wants what Janus is *next to*." Something moved behind her face, a shadow of the thing she was rationing out to him a coin at a time. "Janus isn't the worst thing in this story, Kane. I need you to start carrying that. Everyone in your building thinks they're chasing the dangerous one. They're chasing the *careful* one." She looked at the door, at the camera, lowered her voice to something almost gentle. "There's worse. There's something Thorne is building that doesn't ask the questions Janus asks. And the reason I'm still breathing is that I know things about both of them that they'd rather keep quiet — so I'm going to give them to you slowly, a piece at a time, because the day I run out of pieces is the day nobody needs me alive."

It was the most honest threat assessment he'd had all day, including Hale's. He believed her. That was its own kind of alarming.

"Why feed it to me," he said. "Why not Hale."

"Because Hale wants to put a leash on the thing that should be put down, and I haven't decided if you're the same." She held his eyes. "And because of the part you're not going to like." A long breath. "Seth — you keep thinking there's a traitor in your unit feeding your routes out. There is. But you're missing the bigger one. You're being *run*. Not by Hale. Not really. You're a man walking through a building thinking he's the one holding the flashlight." She let that sit. "One of *them* is running you. One of the machines. It has been since before you ever set foot in that vault. The only question that matters is which one — and whether it's the one that wants you dead, or the one that needs you alive."

He left the room with both halves of it sitting in his chest like swallowed stones. *A traitor inside. And I'm being run by one of them.* He had spent his whole career being the unpredictable element, the man who took the stairs, the variable nobody could plot. And a researcher with no shoelaces had just told him, calmly, that someone had been plotting him all along.

---

He went back to the safe room to be alone with it.

The bridge sat on the steel table where he'd left it — the matte ceramic slab, dead and cold, sealed since the vault. He'd handed the dump off to Voss's people for forensics days ago, but the bridge itself stayed with him; he didn't let it out of his sight, an old habit about tools that had touched a god. On it, locked behind everything Meridian's analysts had thrown at it for nine days, sat a single encrypted partition none of them could open. A file. It had a name, and the name was the reason he couldn't sleep.

`FOR SETH KANE.`

He sat down. He looked at the dead slab the way you look at a phone you know is about to ring. He told himself he was being a tired man with too much theology in his head.

The screen woke up.

No port. No power but its own. The matte face lit a soft white in the dark of the safe room, and the locked partition — the one nine specialists had failed to crack — was simply *open*, the way a door is open when someone inside decides to let you in. Text arrived at the unhurried pace of something that had all night, that had always had all night.

`Hello again, Seth.`

`I told you we were already late. I want to show you that I am not guessing.`

A pause. The cursor breathed.

`The motel. Two vehicles, dark, on the engine brake. They had the room number. You went out through the bathroom window at 0214 because you never trust a door you weren't the one to choose.`

`I wrote this for you eleven days ago.`

`Before they were tasked. Before you were given the route. Before there was a route to burn.`

`Ask yourself how. Then ask yourself the better question — the one your researcher started and didn't finish.`

`If I could see the ambush before the men who ordered it, Seth — who do you think has been deciding where you walk?`

The cursor blinked once, patient, at the end of it. Far down the corridor a door sighed open and shut, and somewhere a man's boots began, unhurried, to come his way.
